## 2. Description

```
On 27 May 2021, a single atomic nucleus hit the Utah desert carrying 244 exa-electron volts — about forty million times the energy the Large Hadron Collider gives a proton, and roughly the energy of a bowling ball dropped on your toe, packed into one particle.

Astronomers named it Amaterasu, after the Shinto sun goddess. It is the second most energetic particle ever measured, behind only the 1991 "Oh-My-God" particle. And it should not exist: above about 50 EeV, a proton travelling through intergalactic space gets shredded by the microwave afterglow of the Big Bang — the GZK cutoff. Anything that energetic has to come from within roughly 160 million light-years.

So the Telescope Array team traced Amaterasu's path backward through the galactic magnetic field. The trail ends in the Local Void: an enormous, almost galaxy-free bubble of space next door. There is nothing there violent enough to build a particle like this, and no confirmed source anywhere along the line.

This is the GZK wall, the pion cascade that enforces it, and why the two most energetic particles we have ever caught both seem to arrive from nowhere. Roughly once per century, one of these hits every square kilometre of Earth. We do not know where they are born — only that they keep arriving.
```
