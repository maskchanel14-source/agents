2. Description (copy-paste)

> *Naegleria fowleri* is a free-living amoeba that lives in warm fresh water — lakes, rivers, hot springs, above 30 °C. You cannot get it by drinking: stomach acid destroys it. It has to go up the nose, along the olfactory nerve, through a plate of bone in the skull, and into the brain.
>
> Then it multiplies, and the brain swells. From first symptom to death: 5.3 days on average. The CDC has recorded 167 US cases since 1962. Four people survived — a fatality rate above 97 %.
>
> In 2016, a 16-year-old in Florida became one of the four. He lived because the treatment started within hours.
>
> Salt water kills the amoeba. The ocean is safe. The lake is not. And it begins as a headache.
>
> Sources: CDC "About Naegleria fowleri" · CDC surveillance summary 1962–2024 · NCBI Bookshelf chapter on *N. fowleri* (5.3-day symptom-to-death, 9.9-day exposure-to-death, treatment protocol). Full claim table and every deliberate omission in sources_and_licenses.md.

5. Pinned comment

> The number that gets lost: 4 survivors out of 167 cases. In 2016 a 16-year-old in Florida became one of them, and the reason he lived is almost uncomfortable — the diagnosis came fast enough that the experimental drug protocol started within hours, and his body was cooled to protect the brain. Most cases are diagnosed after the window has closed.
>
> Important accuracy notes: (1) The CDC figure is "above 97 %", not 99 % — this video uses the real number. (2) You cannot get it from drinking water, and it is not spread person-to-person. (3) The ocean is safe; the amoeba does not survive salt water. (4) The 5.3-day figure is symptom-onset to death; the exposure-to-death figure is 9.9 days and is labelled separately on screen, because conflating them is the most common error in videos on this topic.
>
> Numbers are scary enough on their own. We didn't need to exaggerate. 👇

6. Reply comments (first hour)

1. *"Is it in every lake??"* → "The amoeba is common in warm fresh water; the disease is extremely rare — 167 US cases in 62 years, against hundreds of millions of swims. It is rare because it needs a specific route: forced water up the nose, usually from diving or jumping. There is no honest per-swim risk number, which is why this video doesn't quote one."
2. *"Why is it still 97 % fatal if there's a treatment?"* → "Because the early symptoms — headache, fever, nausea — look like a hundred other things, and by the time the diagnosis is suspected the swelling is already severe. The survivors were treated before that point."
3. *"Can you get it from a neti pot or tap water?"* → "Yes — that's a real route, and it's why health agencies say to use distilled, sterile, or boiled-and-cooled water for nasal rinsing. It is the one route where you can fully control the risk."
